## Are kids allowed on Anchorage glacier helicopter tours?

Generally, yes. Outbound Heli Adventures' family page says it welcomes kids of all ages on its scenic and glacier-landing flights, and the trip is chosen with the parents. Alaska Helicopter Tours lists its year-round Knik Glacier Landing and Grand Knik flights as "all ages."

That makes a helicopter trip a realistic family day out during an Anchorage winter holiday. Three separate questions still need answers:

1. **Can my child fly in the aircraft?** Ask about seating, headsets, passenger weight limits and the operator's own policy.
2. **Can my child safely leave the helicopter and enjoy the landing?** That depends on the weather, the terrain, how much walking is involved and whether the child follows directions.
3. **Can my child join the guided activity?** Trekking, ice climbing and other guided trips can carry their own age, fitness and gear-fit limits, even when the flight that goes with them suits younger children.

Outbound, for example, sets glacier trekking at ages 10 and up because of the microspikes worn on slick ice, and ice climbing at 16 and up because of the strength needed on ice walls. It also says children on guided trips must be large enough to wear the required gear safely. Alaska Helicopter Tours lists its private winter [ice climbing trip](/guides/glacier-ice-climbing-by-helicopter-near-anchorage-winter-beginners/) for ages 13 and up. Other operators use their own thresholds, so never carry one company's minimum age over to another company or product.

A family-friendly helicopter flight does not make every glacier activity suitable for every child.

### Can babies and toddlers fly in a helicopter?

Outbound's family page says babies up to two can fly as lap infants, "just like a regular airline," and that children aged two and over have their own seat. Other Alaska sellers also offer lap infants on some trips, but this is not a universal booking or safety rule.

Keep one safety point in mind. The [FAA recommends a government-approved child restraint over holding a young child on your lap on an aircraft](https://www.faa.gov/travelers/kids-corner). Whether a particular helicopter can take an approved restraint, has the right seat and belt layout, and suits your child's weight or size is a question for that operator. Don't assume an airline car seat, baby carrier or lap hold will work in a tour helicopter.

Outbound states plainly that there are no car seats in its helicopters. If its arrangement does not meet your family's comfort or safety needs, choose another winter activity. A free lap-infant place is no reason to book on its own.

If you use the operator's road shuttle, ask about child car seats separately. Outbound does not provide them on its Anchorage shuttle, so bring your own if your child needs one.

### Can siblings of different ages fly together?

Often, yes, within the aircraft's seats and its weight-and-balance limits. Helicopter seating works differently from picking neighbouring seats on an airliner. Operators need every passenger's weight, and the pilot sets the final seating. On Outbound's flights, each seat takes up to 300 lb, and the combined passenger weight is capped at 650 lb in its R44 helicopter and 900 lb in its R66. A party over the booked aircraft's limit needs a second or larger helicopter, with an extra $350 fee. A family of five or six may be split across two aircraft.

If keeping parents and children in the same helicopter matters, say so when you book, well before check-in. Ask whether the operator can seat your whole family safely in one aircraft or may split the group.

## What a winter glacier helicopter tour feels like for a child

Picture the day from your child's side. First comes the drive to Palmer, a safety briefing and climbing into a helicopter. Then the engine and rotors start, the headset goes on, the aircraft lifts and a snowy valley opens up below. At the end there may be a landing somewhere with no building, toilet or shelter.

The flight is often the highlight. Children may spot braided rivers, mountains, crevasses and ice cliffs. Some look out of the window the whole time; others need reassurance before take-off, or enjoy talking over the headset more than the view.

At the landing, the winter conditions count for more than the photo on the tour website. The ground might be packed snow, rough ice or a landing area beside the glacier. The pilot or guide decides where you can stand and walk. Children need to stay close to their adult and follow the pilot's directions, above all around the aircraft and near drop-offs, holes and weak ice.

It helps to tell children beforehand: *"We will see a glacier from the sky. We may get out to look at the ice, but the pilot chooses where it is safe. We will stay together and listen to the guide."* That prepares them without promising a cave or a landmark.

## How to dress children for a winter glacier landing

A child can be warm enough sitting in the helicopter and still get very cold on open snow or ice. Dress them for the coldest time outside, and expect the car ride to the base to be heated.

### Family winter packing list

- **Thermal base layer:** a wicking top and leggings; avoid cotton next to the skin if the child may sweat.
- **Warm mid layer:** fleece or another warm layer for the day's temperature.
- **Insulated winter jacket:** ideally with a windproof outer.
- **Snow pants:** important for children who kneel, trip or sit in the snow.
- **Waterproof insulated boots:** with good grip and room for warm socks; ask whether the operator's traction aids fit children's boots.
- **Warm socks:** a dry spare pair for younger children if you have room.
- **Gloves or mittens:** bring a spare pair, because wet gloves spoil a winter outing fast.
- **Hat or balaclava:** ask how it has to fit under the operator's headset or helmet.
- **Sunglasses:** snow and ice can be bright even on a cold day.
- **Small snacks and water:** for before or after the flight; follow the operator's rules on food and loose items in the helicopter.
- **Comfort item:** a small toy can calm a nervous child; check it may come on board and keep hold of it around the aircraft.

Plan hearing protection too. Outbound gives children aged two and over their own headset, so they can talk with their parents and the pilot. For babies, or for children who are sensitive to loud noise, agree the exact arrangement with the operator before you book.

Long loose scarves, dangling cords and loose toys are a bad idea around a helicopter. Follow the ground crew's instructions on what you can carry to the aircraft and how to secure it.

## Winter weather and daylight: what families should plan around

Glaciers are there all winter. The planning problem is finding a date when safe flying weather and enough daylight line up with your trip.

Wind, fog, low cloud, snow and icing can delay or cancel flights, and each operator makes its own call. Alaska Helicopter Tours says that when it cancels for weather or safety, it reschedules you and refunds the full price if rescheduling is not possible. Outbound's trip pages also describe rescheduling or refunds when it cancels for weather. If you cancel yourself, different rules can apply.

Leave a spare day in the plan, because moving four or five people to another day is harder than moving one.

### November: early-season conditions and changing snow cover

November can bring winter scenery, but early-season snow and ice vary. A short landing is the simplest choice for a family. Dedicated ice cave trips may not have started yet: Outbound's winter ice cave trip runs from December.

### December: short days and holiday travel

Daylight is short around the winter solstice, which leaves fewer flying hours, especially once you add the drive from Anchorage to Palmer. Don't put a late airport arrival, a long drive and a helicopter flight on one tight day. Confirm holiday operating dates and transport directly with the operator.

### January: deep winter

January can bring deep snow over the mountains and a full winter atmosphere. It is also the month to take gloves, insulated boots and time in the cold seriously, above all with young children. Keep a spare day if the flight is a highlight of the holiday.

### February: more daylight, still winter

Days get longer through February. You may have a little more room for the tour and another activity on the same day, but flights still depend on the weather and on the departure times offered. Exposed blue ice is not guaranteed in February either.

Book the helicopter for an early suitable day of your stay, keep another day free, and don't tie it to a non-refundable flight or a long transfer. ## Is a glacier helicopter tour safe for children?

Any helicopter flight and remote winter landing carries risk, and an operator's "all ages" label does not remove it. What parents can do is pick a suitable trip, use an established operator, follow the crew's instructions and never push a tired or unwilling child into an activity that needs steady feet and attention.

### Before boarding

The operator needs accurate ages and weights to assign the aircraft and seats. Ask about your child's seat, headset or hearing protection, how adults and children will be seated and any help with boarding. Tell a nervous child that headsets and seat belts are part of every flight and that everyone follows the pilot's directions.

### Around the helicopter

Keep each child close to an adult and follow the ground crew. Never walk to or away from the aircraft without the crew's go-ahead. Rotor noise, wind and moving equipment are reasons to keep children together and hold on to loose items.

### At the glacier landing

A glacier is not a groomed snow park. Snow can hide holes, slopes and uneven ice. Stay inside the area the pilot or guide approves, and never let children wander towards crevasses, steep ice walls, cave mouths or water. Ice that looks firm is not safe to explore on your own.

### At an ice cave

Cave conditions change. Even on a trip that includes a possible cave visit, entry always depends on the guide's judgement and that day's conditions. If your youngest child cannot move safely or is uneasy, accept a shorter visit. Never enter an ice cave without a qualified guide's go-ahead.

### When the weather changes

Be ready to cancel or change plans if the operator decides conditions are unsuitable. Don't press the operator to fly because the children have been looking forward to it or because it is your last day.

## Can children get motion sick in a helicopter?

Some can. Turns and unfamiliar movements can bother passengers who are prone to motion sickness, though many people fly without any trouble. If your child gets carsick or worries about motion, tell the operator and talk to your child's doctor about medication before the trip. Never give a child an adult motion-sickness tablet or a sedating product without medical advice for their age.

A short flight is easier to manage than a long trip with several stops. Avoid a heavy meal just before flying, and follow the operator's instructions on snacks, drinks and what to do if someone feels unwell.

## What if a child gets scared during the flight?

It happens, above all with children who find loud noise or seat belts upsetting. Before you go, explain that the helicopter will be loud, that the headset lets everyone talk, and that the pilot may turn to show different views. If you can, find a photo of the aircraft's inside so your children know what to expect.

Bring a small comfort item only if the operator allows it and it can be held securely. Don't promise a frightened child a particular seat, or that the helicopter can land whenever they want; the pilot makes those calls.

With an anxious first-time flyer, start with the shortest suitable flight before a long multi-stop trip.

Tell them the day is about seeing the glacier and learning how the land works, not about reaching a guaranteed cave or doing the longest walk. Give each child a small challenge, such as counting the river's bends or spotting an ice wall from the air.

### A family with teens: try an active trip

**Best fit:** Grand Knik, a dedicated cave trip or guided glacier trekking where the teenagers meet the operator's rules. Outbound sets glacier trekking at 10 and up and ice climbing at 16 and up.

Check that they can walk in traction aids, cope with the cold and follow instructions for several hours. A teen keen to climb or trek may enjoy a guided outing more than another scenic landing.

### Travelling with a baby: start with the seating

Contact the operator first about how it carries infants. Confirm the seating options, noise protection, time in the cold and car seats for the road transfer. If the arrangements don't suit your family, choose a different winter outing and leave the helicopter for a later trip.

## Ten questions to ask before booking a family glacier helicopter tour

Send these to the operator by email or ask them on the phone:

1. What is the minimum age for this exact flight and for its landing or guided activity?
2. Can you safely take our children at their current ages and weights?
3. What is your infant seating policy, and is a seat of their own or an approved restraint possible?
4. Will our children get properly fitted headsets or other hearing protection?
5. Can both adults and all the children fly in the same aircraft?
6. How long is the actual flight, and how long will we be outside at the glacier?
7. Are winter boots, child-sized traction aids or helmets required or provided?
8. If conditions rule out the planned glacier or cave landing, what do we do instead?
9. What is the full price for our family, including transfers, child fares, taxes and any private-flight charge?
10. If weather cancels the flight, or a child falls ill, what refund or change rules apply?

The answers tell you more than a star rating does, because they show whether the trip suits your family on your winter date.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the minimum age for a glacier helicopter tour near Anchorage?

It depends on the operator and the trip; there is no single minimum. Alaska Helicopter Tours lists its Knik Glacier Landing and Grand Knik flights for all ages, and Outbound welcomes children of all ages on scenic and glacier-landing flights after talking the trip through with the parents. Other trips set limits: the AlaskaTours winter flight from Girdwood lists a minimum age of three. Check the exact product you book.

### Can a two-year-old go on a Knik Glacier helicopter tour?

With Outbound, a two-year-old gets their own seat and headset. Confirm the seating and any other requirements for the specific aircraft and operator before you book.

### Are infants free on Alaska helicopter tours?

On some trips, babies under two fly as lap infants, but that is neither a universal fare nor a safety recommendation. Ask what seating or approved restraint options the operator has. The FAA recommends an approved child restraint on aircraft over holding a child on your lap.

### Do children need to wear crampons or ice cleats?

It depends on the route and the trip. On guided winter walks, operators supply traction aids such as microspikes. Ask in advance whether they come in sizes that fit your child's boots, and whether the child can stay near the helicopter if the route doesn't suit them.

### Can children visit ice caves in Alaska in winter?

Some operators sell winter ice cave trips for all ages, such as Outbound's December-to-March trip. A guide still decides on the day whether a safe route exists, and neither cave entry nor particular ice formations are guaranteed. Talk through your youngest child's mobility and listening skills with the operator before you book.

### Is Grand Knik better than the shorter Knik Glacier Landing for kids?

For a first flight or a younger child, the shorter landing is usually the easier day. {{< gyg-link tour="412333" >}}Grand Knik{{< /gyg-link >}} has three stops, costs more and means more time in the aircraft and outside. Older children who love the outdoors may enjoy its variety.

### Can kids do glacier ice climbing near Anchorage?

Older teens can, within each operator's limits. Outbound lists ice climbing for ages 16 and up and its guided trekking for 10 and up; Alaska Helicopter Tours lists its private winter climb for 13 and up. Age is only one part of it: the gear must fit, and the climber needs the strength and focus for the day.

### Are family helicopter tours available in December and January?

Alaska Helicopter Tours and Outbound both fly Knik Glacier landings year-round. A December or January departure still depends on weather, daylight and seats. The winter-only cave and exploration trips have their own seasons: December to March for Outbound's ice cave trip, December 15 to March 14 for Winter Glacier Exploration.

### Can we get to the departure point without a rental car?

Often, yes, with a paid shuttle or a private transfer. Many flights sold as Anchorage trips leave from near Palmer. Confirm pickup coverage, the full transfer charge, the winter schedule and child car seats before you book the flight.

### What happens if bad weather cancels our family flight?

When the operator cancels for unsafe flying weather, it usually offers a new date or a refund. If you cancel, for example because a child is ill or won't fly, different rules may apply. Read your booking's terms and keep a spare day where you can.

### Will our entire family get to sit together?

Not always. The pilot assigns seats for safe weight and balance, and a large family may need more than one aircraft. If one helicopter for everyone matters, get the operator's confirmation before you book.

### Is winter dog sledding a better choice for children?

It depends on what your family wants. Winter dog sledding usually runs on snowy trails near a kennel; the helicopter flight to a dog camp on a glacier is a summer trip. Some winter packages combine a helicopter flight with a kennel visit and a sled ride. Compare the actual itinerary with the photos before you book.
