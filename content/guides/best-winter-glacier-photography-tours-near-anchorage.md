## What can you actually photograph in winter?

The word glacier can suggest one blue wall of ice. Winter glacier photography near Anchorage covers a wide range of subjects, and the ones you see depend on recent snow, sun, altitude and where the pilot can land that day.

### Snow-covered glacier fields and the scale of the landscape

From the helicopter, look for flow patterns, fractures, folds, icefalls and the places where glacier valleys meet. In deep winter, fresh snow turns the scene into large white shapes. That can help you: bare rock gives contrast, dark crevasses make strong lines, and ridges stand out in long shadows.

Include something that shows scale: a distant mountain, the glacier's terminus, a rock wall, a branching valley or the helicopter's shadow. A frame of plain white snow can look flat even when the place overwhelmed you in person.

### Blue ice and towering glacier walls

Glacier ice looks blue because dense ice absorbs the red end of the light spectrum and lets more blue light through. The color is strongest where the ice is clear, thick and exposed; snow on the surface looks white.

In winter you may find blue ice at a wall, a crevasse, a cave entrance or an exposed formation, but not at every landing. A guide or pilot may choose a spot near a wall instead of crossing the glacier surface. Frame blue ice against plain snow or dark rock, so an already blue scene does not end up oversaturated.

For true color, shoot RAW or start from a neutral white balance, and go easy on the blue slider later. Snow in shadow can look blue because the sky lights it; keeping some of that natural contrast looks convincing.

### Ice caves, frozen waterfalls and intricate formations

Ice caves make striking pictures where light reaches through translucent walls. They are not fixed attractions: openings change, collapse, fill with snow or become unsafe. A standard scenic landing does not let you approach or enter a cave. If cave photography is your main aim, book a guided trip and follow the guide's route.

Alaska Helicopter Tours runs <a href="https://www.alaskahelicoptertours.com/winter-glacier-exploration/" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Winter Glacier Exploration</a> as a seasonal guided trip, from $1,049 and sold from December 15 to March 14. The operator says ice cave formations are not always available. For close ice photography, a guided walk gives you far more on foot than a standard landing, where you stay near the helicopter. It is not sold on GetYourGuide or Viator.

### Helicopters, people and dramatic winter backdrops

A helicopter on a snowy landing site shows scale at once. So does one person in a bright jacket against a wide field of snow and ice. Use a wide view for the setting, then a longer focal length for a tighter portrait.

Shoot only from positions the pilot allows, and only after you are told it is safe to move. Never walk behind the aircraft, go near the tail rotor, back away to fit the whole helicopter in, or ask someone to stand somewhere unsafe for a better picture. Rotor wash, slippery ground and hidden ice hazards mean your usual photo habits do not apply near a helicopter.

### Wildlife: a possible bonus, not a photo guarantee

You may see moose, Dall sheep and birds on some flights, but wildlife depends on the route, the season and luck. Don't book a glacier flight as a wildlife photography trip unless the operator sells a wildlife-focused flight. Never ask the pilot to fly close to animals for a picture.

## Best time of day: why low winter sunlight can help

Photographers chase sunrise and sunset light, but around Anchorage in December the sun stays low for most of its short arc. Snow ridges show strong texture and ice surfaces look three-dimensional, even around midday.

Low sun brings its own problems: flare, glare off the snow, and deep shadow in a glacier valley. Mountain ridges can block direct light well before Anchorage's official sunset, and the glacier may look quite different from the heli-base.

### Practical scheduling strategy

1. **Book the flight early in your visit.** A spare day gives you another chance if wind, low cloud or icing cancels the flight.
2. **Ask for a departure in good daylight.** Late morning or around midday often works in December, though the operator sets the times.
3. **Tell the operator photography is your priority.** Ask which trip gives you reasonable ground time and which landing sites are likely, without asking for a route the pilot would not choose.
4. **Don't book a nonrefundable transfer straight after the flight.** Weather can move your departure even if the forecast looked good the day before.
5. **Accept the pilot's decision.** A photograph is never a reason to fly or land in bad conditions.

A sunny forecast in Anchorage does not mean clear flying over Knik Glacier or inside the Chugach.

## Camera settings for glacier photos from a helicopter

A helicopter moves and vibrates, so your settings differ from a tripod landscape shot. Set the camera up before you board; once the best scene appears, you may have little time to change anything.

| Scenario | Suggested starting settings | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Aerial wide landscapes in daylight** | 1/1000 s, f/5.6, Auto ISO, continuous AF | Freezes vibration and movement and keeps a useful landscape aperture |
| **Aerial mountain or ice details** | 1/1250–1/2000 s, f/5.6–8, Auto ISO | Keeps telephoto shots sharp from a moving cabin |
| **Ground-level glacier landscapes** | 1/250–1/500 s, f/8, Auto ISO or a low fixed ISO | Balances depth of field with handheld steadiness |
| **Portraits on a windy glacier** | 1/500–1/1000 s, f/4–5.6, Auto ISO | Freezes flapping clothing, blowing snow and a moving subject |
| **Dim blue ice or shaded formations** | 1/250–1/500 s, a wider aperture if needed, Auto ISO | Keeps handheld shots usable while ISO rises |

*These are starting points. Flight speed, light, focal length, stabilization and your camera's high-ISO performance all change what works. Always follow the operator's rules for cameras and equipment on board.*

### Use shutter speed before you worry about low ISO

A sharp aerial picture at ISO 800 or 1600 usually looks better than a blurred one at ISO 100. On a modern camera, Auto ISO with a fast shutter speed handles the changing light as the helicopter moves from open snow into valley shadow.

For most shots from the aircraft, start around 1/1000 second, and go faster with a longer lens or when nearby terrain races through the frame. Image stabilization helps, but it cannot freeze a moving subject or cancel every vibration.

### Prevent snow from turning grey

Bright snow fools a camera's meter into exposing white scenes too dark. In automatic or semi-automatic modes you may need positive exposure compensation, perhaps +1/3 to +1 stop, after checking the histogram and highlight warnings. The right amount varies: dark rock, bright cloud and backlit snow all change the reading.

Don't add compensation blindly, because it can wipe out fine snow detail. Protect the texture in the highlights, especially where sun hits clean snow, and shoot RAW if your camera can.

### Focus, drive and file format

- **Continuous autofocus:** helps when the aircraft is moving and the framing changes fast.
- **Short bursts:** useful on a moving platform; avoid filling the buffer with hundreds of near-duplicates.
- **RAW capture:** leaves room to adjust white balance, highlights and blue-ice color later.
- **Lens hood and reflections:** use accessories only if they are secure and the crew approves; never press gear against a window where it could damage it or block someone else.
- **No flash through the window:** it bounces off the glass and does nothing for a landscape far below.

### Can you use a polarizing filter through helicopter windows?

Sometimes, but test it first. A circular polarizer can cut some surface reflections. Helicopter windows can also show color bands or stress patterns through one, and a polarizer cuts the light reaching the sensor when you need fast shutter speeds in winter. Polarization also varies across a wide sky, which can leave the image looking uneven.

On a short winter flight, a clean lens, a fast shutter speed, a careful angle to the window and dark clothing that reflects less in the glass usually do more for you than a filter.

## Which lenses should you bring?

Bring the lens you can handle confidently in a small cabin. Several large lenses do not help when you have one seat, little room to move and strict limits on loose gear.

### A versatile zoom is the strongest default

A 24–70 mm, 24–105 mm or similar standard zoom on a full-frame camera is a sensible single lens. It covers wide glacier valleys and middle-distance details without a lens change in flight. On an APS-C camera, the equivalent standard zoom does the same job.

A 16–35 mm wide-angle helps with big snowy terrain and people-in-landscape pictures, especially on the ground. It can make distant mountains look small in an aerial shot, where a longer focal length often shows scale better.

A 70–200 mm telephoto can isolate crevasses, abstract ice lines and layered mountains. It needs faster shutter speeds, can be awkward in a small helicopter, and a casual passenger may not need it.

If you are choosing between a second lens and a spare battery on a short winter trip, take the battery.

### Should you change lenses in the helicopter?

Plan not to. The cabin is small, the ride can be bumpy, and loose lens caps or accessories can become hazards. If the operator allows camera gear, arrive with the lens you plan to use already on the camera.

Ask the operator whether tripods, monopods, camera harnesses, suction mounts, action-camera arms or long lenses are allowed. Don't improvise a mount inside or outside the aircraft; only the operator can approve and secure equipment for flight.

## Aerial photography: window reflections vs doors-off flights

On a normal sightseeing flight, you photograph through the helicopter's windows. They can add reflections, scratches, color casts and distortion, but with care you can still get excellent pictures.

A few simple fixes:

- Wear a dark, non-reflective outer layer to cut reflections in the cabin.
- Hold the camera close to the window without pressing it against the glass, if the crew allows.
- Avoid shooting through strongly angled or visibly distorted parts of the window.
- Watch for your own reflection, your headset or other passengers in the frame.
- Use a faster shutter speed and take several frames as the view changes.

A doors-off flight removes the window, but it is a separate arrangement that has to be set up before take-off. It needs the right aircraft configuration, the operator's approval, secured equipment and passengers ready for much colder air. Outbound lets Chugach Explorer guests ask for doors-off flying; confirm directly whether it can run on your winter date.

Doors-off is not always more comfortable or better for pictures. The cold, the strong airflow and the handling rules can make it harder to work, especially for a beginner.

## Glacier-landing photography: make the most of limited ground time

Time on the ground is short. Take a few minutes to look around, and have a simple shot list ready so you don't spend the whole stop deciding what to photograph.

### Five useful photographs to prioritize

1. **A wide landscape.** Show the snow-covered glacier, the ridgelines and the wider valley.
2. **A person for scale.** Photograph a companion in an allowed spot against the scenery, never near a crevasse or the aircraft.
3. **An ice detail.** Pick out translucent blue sections, cracks, snow texture or frozen shapes visible from the area the guide or pilot allows.
4. **The helicopter in its setting.** Ask the pilot where you may stand and whether you can photograph the aircraft at all.
5. **A contrasting frame.** Change focal length or orientation instead of taking another identical wide shot.

On a short stop, five varied images make a better set than trying to cover everything.

### How to make snow-and-ice photographs more interesting

Snow is visually simple, so look for contrast and structure. Dark rock breaking through a white ridge, a line of crevasses running toward the horizon, or a blue wall framed by deep shadow gives the eye something to follow.

A person in a red or yellow jacket makes a focal point, but don't move them outside the permitted area for the color. Look for foreground texture without crouching over an unknown hole or stepping onto unsupported snow. For panoramas, overlap handheld frames and keep the exposure the same if the light is steady.

### Safety around blue ice and caves

Never go near a crevasse edge or enter an ice cave on your own for a clearer shot. Snow bridges and unstable ice can hide serious hazards, and a camera cannot tell you whether a structure is safe. Ask the guide before leaving the marked path, and accept that some subjects stay out of reach.

If you want a long stretch on foot photographing ice, book a guided winter glacier walk such as Alaska Helicopter Tours' <a href="https://www.alaskahelicoptertours.com/winter-glacier-exploration/" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Winter Glacier Exploration</a>, not a short scenic landing. ## Winter photography packing list

Bring less than you would for a roadside landscape shoot. Everything should work with gloves on, and nothing should need to be set down on the helicopter floor or on windy snow.

**Camera essentials**

- A camera or phone with plenty of storage and a shutter button you can reach easily.
- One practical zoom lens; add a second only if the operator allows it and you will use it.
- A fully charged battery, plus a spare kept warm in an inside pocket.
- A secure strap or tether the operator allows.
- A lens cloth for moisture and fingerprints, somewhere easy to reach.
- A small padded bag that fits the operator's baggage rules.

**Personal winter gear**

- Insulated snow boots with good grip.
- A base layer, a warm midlayer and a windproof insulated outer layer.
- Gloves or mittens that let you work the camera without baring your hands for long.
- A warm hat, and sunglasses for snow glare.
- A small pocket or pouch to secure your phone while you board and get out.

**Keep loose gear secure.** Lens caps, hats, memory cards, small accessories and dropped phones can become serious hazards around a running helicopter. Never go after anything dropped near the rotors. Follow your pilot's instructions at all times.

### Cold batteries and condensation

Cold drains batteries faster for a while. Bring a spare, keep it warm in an inside pocket, and keep playback short outside. Before you come back into a warm vehicle or lodge, put the camera in a closed bag so it warms slowly and less moisture condenses on it.

If condensation does form, don't take the lens off in a warm, damp room. Let the camera warm up fully before you open it.

## Smartphone photography: is an expensive camera necessary?

No. A recent smartphone takes excellent glacier photographs, especially wide landscapes, portraits and short video clips in good daylight. The harder part is keeping the phone secure, the lens clean and the framing deliberate.

Use the main wide camera when light is low. Avoid heavy digital zoom on distant ice. Tap to expose for the bright snow and pull the brightness down if highlights are blowing out. Shoot short clips you will watch again.

Never hold a phone out of an open door or window, and don't use a selfie stick or external mount without the crew's clear approval. If handling a phone in gloves makes you nervous, keep it put away and enjoy the view.

Before you pay a charter deposit, send the operator a short brief covering:

- Your travel dates, and how flexible you are if weather moves the flight.
- Whether you want aerial abstracts, mountain landscapes, landing portraits, blue-ice details or a mix.
- Whether doors-off photography is essential or just preferred.
- How many passengers, roughly what gear, and any very large lenses or stabilizers.
- How many stops you want and the minimum ground time.
- Your budget, and whether you need a fixed total price.

Ask the operator which of these are included, what could prevent them and how a weather change affects the fee. The pilot makes the routing and safety decisions throughout.

## A sample photography-focused half-day itinerary

This is a planning example, not a tour timetable. Build it around the departure you book.

**Late morning:** drive from Anchorage to your Palmer heli-base, with extra time for winter roads.

**Before the flight:** check in and take the safety briefing. Tell the crew which lens you plan to use and ask whether anything needs to be stowed. Set a fast shutter speed, RAW and Auto ISO before you board.

**During the flight:** start with wide mountain scenes, then move to glacier lines and patterns. Change focal length now and then instead of trying to capture every view.

**At the landing(s):** follow every instruction about where to walk. Take a few wide frames, a portrait for scale and some close ice shots where allowed.

**After the flight:** let your gear warm up slowly. Back up your pictures when you can, and keep the rest of the day open in case the flight is delayed or moved.

If you add winter activities later in the day, choose ones that do not depend on the helicopter landing back on time.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are winter glacier helicopter tours near Anchorage good for photography?

For aerial and glacier pictures, yes. You see snow-covered glacier systems, high alpine ridges and ice and rock textures that you cannot reach from the road. Multi-landing tours give you more chances to change the background. Your results depend on the weather, the daylight and where the pilot can safely fly and land.

### Can you photograph blue ice on Knik Glacier in December or January?

Sometimes, where a wall or formation stays exposed, but blue ice is not guaranteed. Fresh snow may cover the glacier surface, and winter pilots may land near an ice wall or at the glacier's edge when the glacier itself is not safe to land on.

### Will I see turquoise glacier pools in winter?

Don't plan on it. Operators tie the bright meltwater pools in their photos to spring and summer. In winter you are more likely to see snow, frozen ground and exposed ice in some places.

### What is the best glacier helicopter tour for a serious photographer?

A multi-landing flight such as Outbound's {{< viator-link code="329560P6" >}}Chugach Explorer{{< /viator-link >}} is a good place to start: Outbound calls it a photographer's flight and lets guests ask for doors-off flying. If you have a specific shot list, a custom flight arranged with the operator may suit you better.

### Are doors-off helicopter photography tours available in winter?

Outbound offers a doors-off option on its Chugach Explorer, but the tour page does not confirm it for any particular winter departure. Ask about the aircraft setup, tethering your gear, clothing, the fee and whether it can run in the expected weather.

### Can I choose my helicopter seat for the best photos?

Don't count on it. The pilot assigns seats for safety, weight and balance, and to keep groups together. You can mention a preference, but no operator can promise a seat that would upset the balance.

### What shutter speed should I use for helicopter photography?

Start at 1/1000 second for aerial scenery in daylight, and go faster with a longer lens or for close terrain moving quickly past. Pair it with Auto ISO and a suitable aperture, take test frames and adjust for the light.

### Is a 70–200mm lens useful?

For isolating ice patterns and stacking mountain layers, yes, but it needs fast shutter speeds and can be awkward in a small cabin. A standard zoom is usually easier on a first glacier flight. Check the operator's equipment rules.

### Is a drone allowed at the glacier landing?

Never assume so. Drones can conflict with helicopter movements, controlled airspace, land-management rules and the operator's own restrictions. Don't bring or fly one without permission from the relevant authorities and the helicopter operator; a passenger booking is not drone authorization.

### Can I walk to a better ice formation after landing?

Only within the area and on the route the pilot or guide allows. Crevasses, snow bridges, unstable ice and the aircraft itself can be hard to judge. No photograph is worth leaving the permitted area.

### Is February better than December for glacier photos?

For scheduling, usually: February has more daylight around Anchorage and still a winter landscape. December can give you moody low-sun pictures but has a shorter flying window each day. Neither month guarantees clear skies or visible blue ice.

### What if cloud or snow cancels my photo flight?

The operator decides on weather and safety. Many offer a new date or a refund when they cancel for unsafe conditions, while your own cancellation follows the booking's terms. Book an early day in your stay and keep a backup date if you can.
